01

Your interview flow

Your central message: “I bring frontline marketplace knowledge, experience identifying unusual customer behaviour, and a financial risk and analytics background. I want to apply those skills to researching buyer risk and making well-supported decisions.”

Use this as a rehearsal sequence for a 30–40 minute interview; the interviewer's actual format may differ.

  • Minutes 0–3 — Introduction: connect your current StubHub/viagogo role to Buyer Fraud.
  • Minutes 3–7 — Motivation: explain why an internal move makes sense and what you understand about the role.
  • Minutes 7–17 — Experience: give one concrete StubHub example and one AIA or accounting example. Aim for 90 seconds each, leaving room for follow-up.
  • Minutes 17–27 — Case exercise: explain your evidence, alternative explanations, next checks, decision and documentation.
  • Minutes 27–33 — Working style: prioritisation, confidentiality, teamwork and learning new fraud tools.
  • Final 5 minutes — Ask two or three questions and close with your contribution.

90-second introduction — resume-grounded draft

“I currently work in Customer Service at StubHub/viagogo, where I started in Seller Support and progressed to Hybrid. I support buyers, fan sellers and brokers across chat, email and phone, so I understand transactions from both sides of the marketplace. My work includes applying policy to complex cases, identifying unusual customer behaviour and escalating potential risk.

“Before StubHub, I worked as a Financial Planner at AIA in Hong Kong, where my responsibilities included compliance and fraud-related checks, reviewing customer and transaction information, and handling sensitive financial details. I also worked in accounting, checking records and identifying discrepancies. My Business Analytics degree supports that practical experience with a structured approach to data and problem-solving.

“I also started crypto mining during university and ran an independent project. I reviewed wallet activity and later assessed counterparties in forum-based fiat-to-crypto and crypto-to-fiat trades. Buyer Fraud is a natural next step because it combines my marketplace experience with transaction review, behavioural analysis and customer communication.”

Employment and responsibility statements come from your sent resume. Individual incidents, decisions, outcomes and performance figures still need your details. Story worksheets need a real incident before you use them as past experience. 03

Your strongest StubHub stories

Use STAR: situation in 15 seconds, task in 10, actions in 45–60, result and lesson in 20. Spend most of your answer on what you personally checked and decided.

Story A — Unusual customer behaviour or an inconsistent account

Best for: “Tell me about a time you identified risk.” This is a worksheet, not a confirmed event.

  • Situation: Choose a real contact where the explanation did not fit the order, notes or account information. What exactly differed?
  • Task: Explain your responsibility and the limit of your authority.
  • Action: Identify the records you actually reviewed, the neutral questions you asked, the alternative explanation you considered, and why you resolved or escalated the case.
  • Result: State only what you observed. If the Fraud Team's conclusion was not visible to you, say so.

Speaking frame: “While supporting [buyer/seller] with [real issue], I noticed [specific inconsistency]. My responsibility was [actual task]. I checked [records available to me] and asked [clarifying question]. I considered [ordinary explanation], but [remaining evidence] meant the concern needed [actual review route]. I documented [key facts] and explained [next step]. The outcome I can confirm was [observed result]. It taught me [lesson relevant to Buyer Fraud].”

Likely follow-up: “What made that suspicious?” Answer with the inconsistency and corroboration, not the customer's tone, language or nationality. “Did you prevent a loss?” Only claim a measured saving if you have a verified figure and attribution.

Story B — A difficult policy decision under time pressure

Best for: customer empathy, judgement and prioritisation. Use a real urgent or complex case; it need not have been fraud.

Speaking frame: “A customer contacted us about [real issue] with [actual deadline]. They wanted [requested outcome], but I needed to follow [applicable policy]. I acknowledged the impact, checked [facts], and explained [available option]. I involved [actual team, if applicable] because [reason], and kept the notes clear so the next person could act. The result was [observed outcome]. This is relevant to Buyer Fraud because urgency should improve my focus without making me skip necessary checks.”

Make it convincing: State the tradeoff. What would have gone wrong if you had acted too quickly? What did you do to avoid unnecessary delay? Avoid saying a customer became satisfied unless you observed that.

If asked for a confirmed fraud case you cannot substantiate

“My experience has been in customer support, identifying potential indicators and using the appropriate escalation route. I have not claimed ownership of the final fraud decision. I can walk you through a real case I handled, explain the evidence available to me and be clear about where my responsibility ended.”

Before rehearsal, fill in: one specific discrepancy; one exact check; one decision you made; one outcome you know. Anonymise all customer details.

“Outside my professional roles, I started cryptocurrency mining during university and ran an independent mining project. I used mining pools across multiple platforms to diversify my exposure. For payments, I checked the activity of the wallet addresses involved, so reviewing the transaction trail was part of how I managed the project.

“Later, I was involved in forum-based markets where people wanted to convert fiat into crypto or crypto back into fiat. That added a counterparty-assessment element. “The connection to Buyer Fraud is combining transaction evidence with customer behaviour. At StubHub, I would apply that approach to the order, account history and approved verification process. A suspicious signal would prompt further investigation; I would look for supporting evidence and legitimate explanations before reaching a decision.”

This answer describes your recurring experience. It does not claim a particular fraud case, rejection, recovered payment or quantified loss prevented.

“How did you review and investigate payments?”

Say: “I tracked the activity of the wallet addresses involved in each payment. Later, with forum-based fiat/crypto transactions, I also reviewed who I was dealing with through seller profiles and behavioural patterns. I checked possible connections to services such as Tornado Cash or similar platforms. That gave me transaction information and counterparty information to consider together. In a Buyer Fraud role, I would use those same two perspectives while following the company's verification and escalation rules.”

Prepare the detail behind those checks:

  • Wallet activity — name the actual coin, wallet or explorer you used and the activity you examined. Explain what was unusual relative to that wallet's history.
  • Possible service links — explain how you identified the link and whether it was direct or through other addresses. Describe the uncertainty; do not identify a person or call funds illicit based solely on a wallet label or mixer connection.
  • Forum profiles — describe what you actually checked. Account history, prior posts, feedback and consistency of the seller's explanation are possible examples, not confirmed methods until you supply them.
  • Behavioural patterns — give one pattern you personally observed and explain why it mattered. Pressure to rush, changing payment details or inconsistent explanations are practice prompts, not reported incidents.
  • Multiple pools — explain how you distributed exposure and why. Diversification addresses concentration risk; it does not establish that a pool is trustworthy or that each payment is safe.
  • Decision and result — give one actual action you took and its known outcome. Distinguish what you observed from what you inferred.

If asked about Tornado Cash or similar services

“A possible link is something to examine in context, not proof that a person committed fraud. I would distinguish a direct interaction from an indirect connection, corroborate the information and be clear about what I could not establish. For StubHub decisions, I would follow the team's approved criteria and review process.”

Turn this into a specific STAR example

Speaking frame: “While reviewing [actual transaction], I noticed [wallet activity or seller behaviour]. I checked [records/tool] and compared that with [profile or communication detail]. I considered [legitimate explanation]. Based on [corroborating evidence], I decided to [actual action]. The result I can confirm was [known outcome]. This shows how I combine transaction records and behaviour without treating suspicion as a conclusion.”

Choose one real transaction before using this as a past-event story. The process is now supported by your account; the individual incident and outcome still need your details.

Technical follow-up — only claim checks you actually used

For Bitcoin-style payments, useful checks include the transaction ID, receiving output/address, amount and confirmations. A transaction may have multiple outputs; its total is not necessarily your payment. Zero confirmations means it has not yet been included in a block. Confirmation rules differ between networks. A confirmed transfer does not establish a person's identity or prove a cardholder authorised a purchase. These are technical preparation points, not additional claims about your past methods. Buyer Fraud case interview

These are hypothetical exercises. They demonstrate your approach, not past incidents. Case routing below is based on the supplied internal guide dated 22 July 2026; confirm current procedures during training.

Case 1 — “I did not authorise this charge”

“First, I would acknowledge the concern and follow the approved identity and information-handling process. I would review the available notes and establish whether this is a charge or an authorisation, whether it relates to a seller fulfilment charge, and whether it is already a chargeback matter. That classification determines the right route.

“For a qualifying unauthorised buyer charge, I would collect the required transaction and callback details through approved channels, document what the customer reported and what the records show, and use the prescribed Buyer Fraud escalation. I would avoid collecting full card details or promising a refund. I would explain the next step and advise the customer to contact their payment provider about securing the affected payment method.”

Why it works: You separate the customer's report from verified findings, gather useful evidence and avoid sending every payment complaint to Fraud.

Case 2 — High-value purchase with inconsistent information

Practice example: An expensive order is associated with a recent account change and a customer explanation that does not match available records. These are prompts for review, not proof.

“I would establish the event deadline and review the information available within my permissions: relevant account history, transaction details, recent changes and prior notes. I would compare the purchase with the account's normal activity and look for corroboration. A gift purchase, travel or a legitimate change could explain some differences.

“I would use the approved verification process, then apply the team's documented criteria and my decision authority. If uncertainty remained beyond that authority, I would escalate with a clear explanation of the evidence and what was still missing. I would record why the decision was proportionate, considering both potential loss and the impact of blocking a genuine buyer.”

If asked about social media: “The job description mentions social platforms. I would use only approved methods and relevant information, corroborate it with other evidence and avoid treating an absent profile as proof of fraud.”

Case 3 — Tickets moved to an unknown email; event in six hours

“I would distinguish a delivery or re-transfer issue from confirmed account takeover. The supplied guide routes re-transfer complaints through the delivery workflow; the unknown email alone does not establish ATO. I would review the notes for Fraud Team confirmation and any relevant account block. For confirmed ATO, I would follow the documented account-protection and compensation routes. An unresolved qualifying Buyer Fraud case with an event within 24 hours meets the supplied guide's supervisor urgency criterion. I would document the evidence and event time without promising an unblock or refund.”

Routing judgement and communication

This section summarises the supplied internal reference, not a live policy lookup. Specialist Buyer Fraud decision authority may differ from frontline support routing.

Distinctions worth practising

  • Chargeback status or dispute: route to Chargebacks under the supplied guide. Do not treat it as a general Buyer Fraud escalation.
  • Automated payment review or bank authorisation hold: follow the documented payment-state guidance. Do not promise an expedited bank release or assume an automated decline proves fraud.
  • Accidental purchase or acknowledged friend/family purchase: the supplied guide describes the applicable sales/relisting route, not automatic Buyer Fraud escalation. Check current policy for the actual facts and market.
  • Direct buyer-to-seller contact: the supplied guide distinguishes a first occurrence without proof from repeat contact or existing proof. Document, apply the appropriate warning, and escalate when its conditions are met.
  • Fake support number, employee impersonation or phishing: preserve relevant evidence through approved channels, follow the applicable safety route and discourage further interaction with the suspicious contact.
  • Verification text: confirm the message against the approved process before reassuring a customer. Do not describe every message bearing a company name as safe.

A concise handoff

“Customer reports [claim]. Records show [facts and timestamps]. I checked [sources] and completed [actions]. The unresolved concern is [specific discrepancy]. Relevant urgency is [event date/time or other qualifying condition]. Requested next action is [review]. Contact details and supporting evidence are recorded in the approved fields.”

Keep fact, customer statement and inference distinct. Include the actual missing evidence; avoid labels such as “obvious fraudster.”

Customer communication during a review

“I understand why this is concerning, especially with the event approaching. I need to check the information through our approved process. I have documented the issue and will explain the next step available to us. I cannot confirm the outcome before the review is complete.”

For a potential phishing message: “Please avoid the links in that message and use the official app or website to contact support. We can check the concern through the approved channel.” StubHub's public safety guidance also advises caution with unsolicited links and off-platform payments. [Public source: S3]

When the procedure is unclear

“I would check the current knowledge article and ask a supervisor where the guidance is ambiguous. I would still document the evidence and urgency so the case can move forward.”

The supplied extract uses both “3–4 days” and “3–4 business days,” and its phishing-forwarding destinations are incomplete. It also distinguishes several review states. Do not invent a mailbox, promise an exact SLA from this extract, or use a blanket rule that all cases under 24 hours must wait. Follow the current article for the specific scenario.

08

Follow-up questions and persuasive answers

Why choose you over someone with more fraud experience?

“I bring working knowledge of the marketplace from both buyer and seller support, experience applying policy to complex contacts, and a financial risk and analytics background. That gives me a useful foundation for reviewing evidence and communicating with customers. I would still need to learn the team's fraud tools and decision standards. I would approach that through training, feedback and consistent case review.”

How do you balance fraud prevention and customer experience?

“I would use the evidence to decide what level of review is justified, rather than treating one unusual detail as a verdict. Missing fraud creates losses, but unnecessary declines also harm genuine customers. I would follow the team's criteria, use approved verification, explain the process clearly and document the reasoning so decisions can be reviewed.”

How would you prioritise a busy queue?

“I would use the team's priority rules, considering the potential harm, event deadline, age of the case and what action is still possible. I would check whether a time-sensitive case needs supervisor attention while keeping ownership and next steps visible for the rest of the queue. In support, I already work across multiple channels; I would adapt that organisation to the fraud queue's standards.”

What if you disagree with another agent?

“I would compare the evidence and the applicable policy with them, explain the particular detail driving my concern, and listen for information I missed. If the decision remained unclear or exceeded my authority, I would ask the appropriate reviewer. I would keep the notes factual so the eventual decision is easy to understand.”

Tell me about a mistake

Choose a real, bounded mistake. Explain what you missed, how you corrected it, who needed to know and what check you changed afterward. Do not invent an incident. A useful frame is: “I initially [actual mistake]. I realised it when [signal], corrected it by [action], and now [specific prevention step].”

How would you share a new fraud pattern?

“I would first distinguish a repeated, evidenced pattern from unrelated cases. I would summarise the common features, the number of relevant examples, the uncertainty and any legitimate explanation. I would share it through the approved team channel with references colleagues can review, without including unnecessary customer information.”

What would you focus on in your first 30–60–90 days?

“First I would learn the tools, policies, decision limits and quality expectations through training and reviewed cases. Next I would focus on handling my assigned cases consistently and applying feedback. As I become reliable, I would look for recurring issues I could document for the team. I would agree targets with my manager rather than assume a particular case volume or loss-reduction goal.”

Availability

The supplied job description includes weekends and flexible working times. Prepare a truthful statement of your availability and any constraints; do not promise a schedule you cannot meet.

  • My AIA and accounting experience support risk awareness and careful checks.
  • I distinguish a concern from a conclusion and act within my authority.

Questions for the interviewer

Choose two or three, depending on what the interview has already covered.

  • “What distinguishes a strong Buyer Fraud agent in the first three months?”
  • “How do you evaluate decision quality alongside speed and the effect on genuine buyers?”
  • “How are new agents trained and calibrated on difficult or ambiguous cases?”
  • “Where could someone coming from Seller Support and Hybrid contribute most quickly?”
  • “How does the team share emerging patterns and feedback with Customer Service?”

30-second closing

“This role brings together the areas I want to build on: marketplace knowledge, customer judgement and analytical investigation. Through StubHub support, I understand the importance of timely, clear communication and consistent policy decisions. My earlier financial and accounting experience adds careful evidence review and confidentiality. I would be motivated to learn the team's tools and help protect customers and the business through well-supported decisions.”

  • Pick two real incidents: one StubHub case and one AIA or accounting case.
