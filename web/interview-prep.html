Fraud case practice

Hypothetical cases. Routing reflects the supplied 22 July 2026 guide; check current procedures during training.

“I did not authorise this charge.”

I would acknowledge the concern and follow the approved identity checks. I would establish whether this is a buyer charge, an authorisation hold, a seller fulfilment charge or an existing chargeback.

For a qualifying unauthorised buyer charge, I would collect the required details through approved channels, document the customer's report and escalate to Buyer Fraud. I would explain the next step and advise them to contact their payment provider to secure the payment method.

  • Classify the issue before routing.
  • No full card details or promised refunds.

High-value order with inconsistent information

I would check the event deadline, account history, transaction details, recent changes and prior notes. I would look for supporting evidence and consider explanations such as a gift purchase or travel.

I would follow approved verification and decision criteria. If the uncertainty exceeded my authority, I would escalate with the evidence, missing information and urgency clearly documented.

  • One unusual detail is a reason to check, not a verdict.
  • Balance potential loss with the impact on a genuine buyer.

Tickets sent to an unknown email; event in six hours

I would check whether this is a delivery issue or confirmed account takeover. I would review the notes for Fraud Team confirmation and any account block. Re-transfer complaints follow the delivery workflow; confirmed takeover follows the account-protection and compensation routes.

An unresolved qualifying Buyer Fraud case with an event within 24 hours meets the supplied guide's supervisor urgency criterion. I would flag the event time and evidence without promising an unblock or refund.

  • Unknown email alone does not confirm account takeover.
  • Check the case type, confirmation and urgency.

Useful algorithms for buyer fraud

Proposed interview ideas for a ticket marketplace, not claims about StubHub's internal systems. Examples are hypothetical. Signals depend on approved data access and team policy.

Your 45-second answer

I would start with velocity rules to spot bursts of payment attempts, then a risk model that combines several signals. Graph analysis could help connect related accounts, while behaviour changes could flag possible account takeover. For tickets, I would also prioritise review by the event deadline and what action is still possible. I would measure fraud losses alongside genuine buyers incorrectly stopped. These are approaches I would like to learn; I have not deployed them in my current role.

1. Sliding-window velocity rules: spot rapid attempts

Count attempts, failed payments and distinct payment tokens per account or device over recent time windows. A sudden burst across several accounts could trigger review or approved additional verification.

  • Ticket example: one device repeatedly tries different payment methods for high-value tickets shortly before an event.
  • Watch out: a popular ticket release, payment outage or shared household can create legitimate bursts. Compare with the event's normal traffic; do not block on an IP address alone.

Velocity means how much activity happens in a short time. I would check whether the burst has other risk indicators before treating it as suspicious.

2. Logistic regression or gradient-boosted trees: combine signals

Start with logistic regression as an interpretable baseline; compare it with a boosted-tree model such as XGBoost, which can capture interactions. Possible inputs include account age, recent payment failures, order value relative to past purchases, device changes and time until the event. Train against carefully reviewed historical outcomes.

  • Ticket example: a new account buying expensive tickets is not enough by itself; several independent indicators could raise the review priority.
  • Watch out: a score estimates risk, not guilt. Validate and calibrate it before interpreting it as a probability. Keep unauthorised-payment fraud separate from non-delivery disputes.

A model can combine weak signals into a more useful risk estimate. I would use the reasons behind the score to guide my investigation.

3. Graph analysis: connect related cases

Represent accounts, tokenised payment identifiers and devices as nodes, with edges for observed use. Connected components or community detection can surface groups for investigation; stronger, repeated links deserve more weight than a shared public network.

  • Ticket example: several recently created buyer accounts share a device and payment token, with links to previously confirmed fraud.
  • Watch out: families and shared devices create legitimate links. Association is a lead, not proof; inspect the underlying cases and timestamps.

I would look beyond one order to see whether related accounts show the same pattern, then verify the connections.

4. Sequence rules and change-point detection: flag account takeover

Compare recent account activity with its history. Sequence rules can identify a new-device login followed by a password or contact change and a purchase; change-point detection can flag a sustained shift in behaviour for review.

  • Ticket example: an established account changes its email and then buys tickets for an imminent event from a new device.
  • Watch out: travel, a replacement phone or a legitimate gift can explain changes. Approved identity checks and supporting evidence remain essential.

The sequence of changes may be more informative than any one change. I would check whether the activity fits the account's usual behaviour.

5. Isolation Forest: discover unfamiliar patterns

Isolation Forest flags observations that are easier to separate from the rest of the data. It can help find unusual combinations when confirmed fraud labels are limited. Compare similar event and buyer cohorts so ordinary differences do not dominate.

  • Ticket example: an unusual combination of purchase frequency, value and account changes enters an analyst discovery queue.
  • Watch out: unusual does not mean fraudulent. Use this for investigation and pattern discovery, not automatic rejection.

Anomaly detection could help discover a new pattern, but a reviewer still needs to establish what actually happened.

6. Cost-sensitive prioritisation: review the right case in time

Rank cases using estimated preventable loss, event urgency, case age and review effort. A priority queue can surface urgent cases while an ageing rule prevents older cases from being ignored. Event time affects the review deadline, not whether a buyer is guilty.

  • Illustrative calculation: a calibrated 10% fraud probability × $500 net loss if fraudulent = $50 expected fraud loss. This alone does not justify a block: compare the likely benefit of each action with review cost, customer friction and false declines.
  • Ticket example: escalate a risky order for an event in two hours when an authorised intervention is still possible, following the team's priority rules.

I would combine risk with urgency and the available action, so the team can protect customers before the event deadline.

How would you know these ideas work?

  • Use realistic labels: allow time for disputes to mature; distinguish confirmed fraud, legitimate orders and service-related disputes. Unresolved cases are not automatically legitimate.
  • Avoid hindsight: train on older cases and test on later ones, using only information available at the decision time. Never use a later chargeback as an earlier checkout feature.
  • Measure both sides: precision is the share of flagged cases that are fraud; recall is the share of actual fraud caught. Also track fraud loss per sales volume, genuine-buyer false declines, checkout completion and review time before the event.
  • Handle rare fraud: accuracy can look high even when every fraud case is missed. Compare precision and recall at the team's actual review capacity; check calibration after class weighting or resampling.
  • Validate safely: compare with existing rules in shadow mode, review false positives and monitor changes across events, new buyers and returning buyers. Use approved, minimised data and human review for ambiguous cases.

Which would you suggest first?

I would begin with understandable velocity rules and a clear review queue, then test a simple risk model against that baseline. Graph and anomaly methods would be useful additions if the data and team capacity support them. My contribution would be spotting patterns from frontline cases, documenting evidence and feeding confirmed outcomes back to the team.

Final review

Five points to remember

  • I know both sides of the marketplace.
  • I check customer explanations against records.
  • I bring financial risk awareness and analytical skills.
  • I separate suspicion from evidence.
  • I communicate clearly and work within my authority.

Questions to ask: choose two or three

  • What does success look like in the first three months?
  • How do you balance decision quality, speed and customer impact?
  • How are new agents trained on difficult cases?
  • Where could my Seller Support and Hybrid experience help most?

Closing script

This role combines my marketplace knowledge, customer experience and interest in investigation. I would bring careful evidence review, clear communication and a willingness to learn. I am excited about the opportunity to help protect customers and the business.
